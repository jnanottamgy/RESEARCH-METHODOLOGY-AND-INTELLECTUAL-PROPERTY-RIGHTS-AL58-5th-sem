Q1UNIT I
Briefly explain the different types of research.
6 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q1aSlides 19–21

Research is classified in pairs, depending on its purpose, the kind of data it uses, and where it is carried out.

1. Descriptive vs Analytical. Descriptive research includes surveys and fact-finding enquiries; it describes the state of affairs as it exists, and the researcher has no control over the variables (e.g. a survey of how many students use UPI daily). In analytical research the researcher uses facts or information already available and analyses them to make a critical evaluation (e.g. analysing published accident data to find the main causes).
2. Applied vs Fundamental. Applied (action) research aims at finding a solution for an immediate problem facing society or an industrial or business organisation (e.g. reducing the rejection rate on a production line). Fundamental (basic or pure) research is concerned with generalisations and the formulation of a theory (e.g. studying the properties of graphene).
3. Quantitative vs Qualitative. Quantitative research is based on the measurement of quantity or amount (e.g. battery life in hours). Qualitative research is concerned with phenomena relating to quality or kind, such as motives, opinions and attitudes (e.g. why customers prefer a brand).
4. Conceptual vs Empirical. Conceptual research relates to some abstract idea or theory; thinkers use it to develop new concepts or reinterpret existing ones. Empirical research is data-based: its conclusions can be verified by observation or experiment, so it is also called experimental research.
5. Some other types.
  • One-time vs longitudinal: confined to a single time period vs carried on over several time periods.
  • Field-setting, laboratory or simulation: classified by the environment the research is done in.
  • Clinical or diagnostic: uses case-study or in-depth methods to reach basic causal relations.
  • Conclusion-oriented vs decision-oriented: in the first, the researcher is free to pick a problem and redesign the enquiry as they go; the second serves the needs of a decision-maker.

Q2UNIT I
Why is ethics important in research? What key ethical considerations should researchers keep in mind?
6 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q2aSlides 22–24

Definition. Ethics in research provides guidelines for the responsible conduct of research: what researchers should and should not do while planning, doing and reporting research.

Why ethics is important
  • Protects the integrity of research. It prevents fabrication, falsification and plagiarism, so the research record stays true.
  • Builds trust and credibility. The public, funders and other researchers rely on published results.
  • Prevents harm. Misconduct can cause harm to people and the environment, wastes resources and damages the credibility of research.
  • Supports collaboration. Fairness, mutual respect and honest credit-sharing let teams work together.
  • Ensures accountability. Research uses public money and must follow laws and institutional policies.

Q3UNIT I
What is research misconduct? Explain its types and the common forms of plagiarism.
5 M★★ Very likelySlides 23–25

Definition. Research misconduct is actions or questionable research practices that fall short of the standards of ethics, research and scholarship required to uphold the integrity of research. It harms people and the environment, wastes resources, undermines the research record and damages credibility.

Types of misconduct: FFP
Fabrication: making up data or results and recording or reporting them (e.g. inventing readings for a test never performed).
Falsification: manipulating research materials, or changing or omitting data or results, so the research is not accurately represented (e.g. deleting points that don't fit the hypothesis).
Plagiarism: the appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results or words without giving appropriate credit.

Q4UNIT I
List and explain the three main functions of citation.
6 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q3aSlides 33–35, 41–42

Citations (references) credit others for their work while allowing readers to trace the source publication. Each source is cited twice: (1) an in-text citation exactly where it is quoted or paraphrased, and (2) an entry in the reference list at the end of the article or chapter.

1. Verification function. Authors may make intentional or unintentional distortions or misleading statements. A citation lets readers check whether the original source justifies the claim, and whether the claim is properly described in the present work.
2. Acknowledgment function. Researchers primarily receive credit for their work through citations. Citations play a crucial role in promotion and continued employment, and organisations award research funding based on reputation. They enhance a researcher's reputation and give the background of the work.
3. Documentation function. Citations document scientific concepts and the historical progress of a technology over the years.
Memory lineVery Accurate Data
Verification · Acknowledgment · Documentation
When citations fail their purpose (bonus point)
  • Spurious: citing work you haven't read, or that isn't relevant.
  • Biased: citing only work that supports your view, or your friends' work.
  • Self-citations: excessive citing of your own work to inflate counts.
  • Coercive: an editor or reviewer pressuring authors to cite their journal or work.

Q7UNIT I
Your team must frame a title for its research project. What will you consider? (Also asked as: "Explain the impact of title and keywords on citations.")
3–4 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q2cSlides 36–38

The citation rate of a paper depends on the significance and availability of the journal, the publication type, the research area and the importance of the work. It also depends on the length of the title, the type of title and the keywords chosen. The title is the most important attribute of a paper: it markets the paper and makes it traceable.

  1. Informative and accurate. It should represent the paper effectively and match its scope: problem + method (+ application).
  2. Type of title. Descriptive (states the topic), declarative (states the main finding) or interrogative (asks a question). Choose deliberately.
  3. Length. Concise. Cut filler like "A study on…", but don't make it so short that it's vague.
  4. Specific markers. A colon or hyphen to add a subtitle; a question mark for an interrogative title.
  5. Keywords. Use the words people actually search. Search engines, journals, digital libraries and indexing services use keywords to categorise the work and direct it to the relevant audience.
  6. Gains attention, without hype. Catchy but not misleading. Avoid jargon and unexplained abbreviations.

Q1UNIT II
Define: variable, independent variable, dependent variable, extraneous variable, confounded variable, continuous and discrete variables.
5 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q1bSlides 5–10 + Kothari
Variable: any characteristic which is subject to change and can have more than one value (e.g. intelligence, motivation, age, gender).
Independent variable (IV): the factor the researcher manipulates or controls to observe its effect: the "cause". It comes in two types: experimental IV, directly manipulated (dosage of a drug), and subject variable, a characteristic that can't be manipulated but categorises participants (age, gender).
Dependent variable (DV): the outcome measured in response to changes in the IV: the "effect" (test scores, growth, behavioural response).
Extraneous variable: any variable other than the IV that could influence the DV. It adds noise (temperature, lighting, mood, fatigue, time of day).
Confounded variable: when an extraneous variable correlates with both the IV and the DV, their separate effects cannot be separated (e.g. testing a new teaching method without controlling for students' prior knowledge).
Continuous variable: can take any value in a range, including decimals (age, height, weight).
Discrete variable: takes only whole-number (integer) values (number of children, number of defects).

Q3UNIT II
What is a research hypothesis? State its purpose, and explain null vs alternative and directional vs non-directional hypotheses with examples.
6 M★★★ BankerSlides 11–16

Definition. A research hypothesis is a predictive statement that relates an independent variable to a dependent variable. It is a proposition or set of propositions set forth as an explanation for some phenomena, either as a provisional conjecture to guide investigation, or accepted as highly probable in the light of established facts.

Purpose of a hypothesis
  • Guides and gives direction to the study
  • Defines which facts are relevant and not relevant
  • Suggests the most appropriate research design
  • Provides a framework for organising conclusions
  • Limits the research to a specific area
  • Offers explanations for relationships that can be empirically tested
  • Shows the researcher has enough background knowledge
  • Structures the next phase, giving continuity

Q4UNIT II
State the principles of experimental design. Explain the Completely Randomised Design (CRD) with an example.
6 M★★ Very likelySlides 25–32

Experimental design methods help the experimenter understand and evaluate the factors that influence a system using statistical approaches.

Principles of experimental design
  • Control: keep conditions as similar as possible for all treatment groups, apart from the treatments themselves. Then any difference is due to the treatment.
  • Randomisation: use chance to assign units to treatments. This balances the effect of uncontrolled ("lurking") variables.
  • Replication: repeat the experiment on many subjects or locations.
Memory lineCats Run Rapidly
Control · Randomisation · Replication
Memory lineCool Rabbits Love Food
CRD · RBD · LSD (Latin square) · Factorial
Completely Randomised Design (CRD)

Treatments are assigned to experimental units completely at random, so each unit has an equal chance of receiving any treatment and the treatment effects can be assessed without bias. Statistical model: Yij = μ + Ti + eij (observation = overall mean + treatment effect + random error).

Slide example · baking powderHypothesis: adding more baking powder increases cake height. Treatments: ¼, ½, ¾ and 1 tbsp. Cakes are assigned to the four levels completely at random, baked, and their heights compared.
Characteristics and advantages
  • Random assignment
  • Single-factor focus
  • Simplicity of layout and analysis
  • Maximum degrees of freedom for error
  • Flexible: any number of treatments and replications
Memory lineRohit Smashes Sixes, Mesmerises Fans
Random assignment · Single-factor focus · Simplicity · Maximum degrees of freedom for error · Flexible
Limitations
  • Needs homogeneous units
  • Less accurate in field experiments
  • Can't detect interactions between factors
Memory lineHot Fresh Idli
Homogeneous units needed · Field experiments: less accurate · Interactions not detected

Used in: laboratory experiments, controlled agricultural tests, clinical trials, basic biology and chemistry.

6 M★★★ BankerPYQ 2024 · Q2bSlides 46–50

A factorial design evaluates the effects of two or more independent variables (factors) on a dependent variable. It measures the main effects (each factor's individual impact) and the interaction effects (how one factor's effect changes with the level of another). Number of combinations = product of the levels.

Simple factorial: two factors, each at two levels (2 × 2). Example: drug dosage (low, high) × duration (short, long) gives 4 combinations: low-short, low-long, high-short, high-long.
Complex factorial: more than two factors, or factors with more than two levels (2 × 3, 3 × 2 × 2). Example: 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 combinations:

Q4UNIT III
What is sampling? Why is it needed? Define universe, sampling frame, sample design, statistic and parameter, sampling error, precision, confidence level and significance level.
6 M★★★ BankerSlides 20–26

A complete enumeration of all items in the population is a census inquiry. Sampling is the selection of some part of an aggregate or totality on the basis of which a judgement or inference about the aggregate is made: obtaining information about the whole population by examining only part of it.

Need for sampling
  1. Saves time and money.
  2. Can give more accurate measurements when done by trained investigators.
  3. The only way when the population is infinite.
  4. The only choice for destructive testing (for example, the life of light bulbs).
  5. Lets you estimate the sampling errors.
Memory lineTea And Idli Daily, Enjoy
Time and money saved · Accuracy · Infinite population · Destructive testing · Estimate sampling error
Important definitions
Universe or populationThe universe is the total of items in any field of inquiry; the population is the total of items about which information is desired.
Sampling frameA list of all sampling units from which the sample is drawn (for example, a telephone directory for a city opinion survey). It should represent the population well.
Sample designA definite plan for obtaining a sample from the sampling frame.
Statistic vs parameterA statistic describes a sample (sample mean); a parameter describes the population (population mean).
Sampling errorThe inaccuracy that comes from studying only part of the population (also called error variance).
PrecisionThe range within which the population value will lie. For an estimate of ₹4000 with ±4% precision, the true value is ₹3840 to ₹4160.
Confidence and significance levelConfidence: the expected % of times the actual value falls within the precision limits. At 95% confidence, the significance level is 100 − 95 = 5%.
Sampling distributionThe means (or other statistics) of many samples, with their frequencies. Its mean equals the population mean.

Criteria for selecting a sampling procedure

Sampling involves two costs: the cost of collecting data and the cost of an incorrect inference. Wrong inferences come from two causes: systematic bias and sampling error.

Causes of systematic bias:
  1. Inappropriate sampling frame: a biased representation of the universe
  2. Defective measuring device: constantly in error
  3. Non-respondents: not all selected individuals can be sampled
  4. Indeterminacy principle: people behave differently when observed
  5. Natural bias in reporting data by respondents
Memory lineI Do Not Invite Nonsense
Inappropriate sampling frame · Defective measuring device · Non-respondents · Indeterminacy principle · Natural bias in reporting
Sampling error is the random variation of sample estimates around the true population parameter. Measuring it gives the precision of the sampling plan. Increasing the sample size improves precision, but it also raises cost and enhances systematic bias. The effective fix is a better sampling design with smaller error for the same size and cost.
Systematic biasSampling error
Caused by errors in the sampling procedureCaused by random chance
Not reduced by a bigger sampleReduced by a bigger sample or a better design

Sample designs are classified on two bases: the representation basis (probability or non-probability) and the element selection technique (unrestricted, where each element is drawn individually from the population at large, or restricted, meaning all other forms).

Element selection ↓ · Representation →Probability samplingNon-probability sampling

Memory lineSweet Chai Cools People
Simple random (probability, unrestricted) · Complex random (probability, restricted) · Convenience (non-probability, unrestricted) · Purposive (non-probability, restricted)
Non-probability sampling (also called deliberate, purposive or judgement sampling): there is no basis for estimating the probability of each item being included. Items are selected deliberately by the researcher, whose choice is supreme. Example: a few towns and villages chosen to represent a whole state. Risk: the personal element (organiser's bias) enters, and sampling error cannot be estimated. It suits small inquiries because it saves time and money. Quota sampling is an example: interviewers fill quotas from different strata at their own discretion. It is cheap and convenient, but not random.
Probability sampling (also called random or chance sampling): every item of the universe has an equal chance of inclusion, like a lottery, so blind chance alone decides. Errors of estimation can be measured, and it obeys the law of statistical regularity: a random sample has the same composition as the universe. That makes it the best technique for a representative sample. Without replacement, a selected item can't appear again; with replacement, it is returned and could appear twice.

Important sampling distributions

5 M★★Slides 30–35
  1. Of the mean: the distribution of means of all possible random samples of a given size. It is normal if the population is normal, and near-normal for large samples.
  2. Of the proportion: for attribute data (defective or non-defective). It is binomial, becoming normal as n grows.
  3. Student's t: used when the population SD is unknown and n < 30. Symmetrical; approaches normal as n grows.
  4. F: compares the variances of two samples from two normal populations with the same variance.
  5. Chi-square: not symmetrical, all values positive. Used for observed vs expected frequencies and goodness of fit.

Central Limit Theorem and Standard Error

4 M★★Slides 36–37

CLT: whether or not the population is normal, the means of samples drawn from it follow a normal distribution when n > 30. If the population is normal, sample means are normal for any n.

Standard error (SE): the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic. For the mean, SE = σ / √n. It:

  • tests whether a difference could be due to chance
  • gives an idea of the reliability and precision of a sample
  • sets the limits within which the population parameter is expected to lie

Honesty · Objectivity · Integrity · Carefulness · Openness · Respect for IP · Confidentiality

Misconduct

Fake Facts Punished Fabrication · Falsification · Plagiarism. Not honest error.

Citation functions

Very Accurate Data Verification · Acknowledgment · Documentation

Literature review

Existing knowledge gives: context · significance · originality · tools.
Prior art, 5 steps: hypothesis → models and conditions → connections → compare and contrast → strong points and loopholes

Title

3 aspects: type · length · specific markers, plus keywords. Title = marketing + traceability. Critical reading finds mistakes (easy); creative reading finds ideas (hard).

Variables

IV = cause (experimental or subject) · DV = effect · Extraneous = other influence · Confounded = can't be separated · Continuous = decimals · Discrete = integers · Control = minimise extraneous

Hypothesis

H0: μ1 = μ2 (no effect) · H1: μ1 ≠ μ2 · Directional: μ1 < μ2 · Non-directional: ≠
By formulation vs by direction

Design principles

Cats Run Rapidly Control · Randomisation · Replication
Blocking ladder: CRD 0 → RBD 1 → LSD 2 blocking factors · Factorial = 2+ factors

Models

CRD: Y = μ + T + e
RBD: Y = μ + T + B + E
LSD: Y = μ + T + R + C + E
Factorial: combinations = product of the levels (3 × 2 × 2 = 12)

Slide examples

CRD: baking powder (¼ to 1 tbsp) · RBD: fertilisers × crops · LSD: strawberries (drip, overhead, none; moisture × nitrogen) · Factorial: dosage × duration (2 × 2); therapy × duration × gender (3 × 2 × 2)

Research design

Need: smooth sailing · advance planning · reliability · direction · decisions · no blind search · anticipate problems
Good design: minimum bias, maximum reliability, flexible, controls extraneous variables

Need for sampling

Time and money · accuracy · infinite population · destructive testing · estimate sampling error
Precision: ₹4000 ± 4% = ₹3840 to ₹4160 · 95% confidence = 5% significance

Systematic bias

I Do Not Invite Nonsense
Inappropriate frame · Defective device · Non-respondents · Indeterminacy · Natural bias
A bigger sample fixes sampling error, not bias

Good sample design

Representative · small sampling error · within funds · bias controlled · generalisable with confidence
Steps: universe → unit → frame → size → parameters → budget → procedure

Sample types (2 × 2)

Probability + unrestricted = simple random · probability + restricted = complex random (cluster, systematic, stratified)
Non-probability + unrestricted = convenience · non-probability + restricted = purposive (quota, judgement)
